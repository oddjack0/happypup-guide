Puppy Training Basics: The First Two Weeks

A new puppy is not a blank slate you need to drill — they are a baby who is learning how your house works. The first two weeks are about three simple skills, lots of sleep, and hundreds of tiny rewards for getting things right.

What a young puppy can actually learn

Between roughly 3 and 16 weeks, puppies go through a sensitive socialisation period: new people, sounds, surfaces and experiences are filed away as “normal and safe” far more easily than later in life. Your vet can advise on safe socialisation around your puppy’s vaccination schedule — carried introductions and qualified puppy classes are usually the answer, rather than avoiding the world.

Formal skills matter too, but keep expectations puppy-sized. An 8-to-12-week-old can learn their name, a sit, and the beginnings of coming when called. What they cannot do is concentrate for long, hold their bladder for hours, or understand why you are cross about something that happened ten minutes ago. Train the puppy in front of you, not the adult dog you are imagining.

The three rules that make training work

  1. Reward fast. Deliver a tiny treat or praise within one or two seconds of the behaviour you liked, or your puppy will connect the reward to whatever they did next. A marker word like “Yes!” — said the instant they get it right, then followed by the treat — makes the timing much easier.
  2. Keep sessions tiny. One to five minutes, several times a day, beats one long lesson. End while your puppy is still having fun, and let them nap afterwards: puppies need 18–20 hours of sleep a day, and a lot of “naughty” evening behaviour is simply an overtired puppy.
  3. Be consistent. Everyone in the house should use the same cue words and the same rules. If “off” means get off the sofa on Monday and nothing on Tuesday, your puppy is not being stubborn — they are confused.

Teach the first three skills

1. Their name

Say your puppy’s name once, cheerfully. The moment they look at you, say “Yes!” and give a treat. Repeat five or six times, a few times a day, in different rooms. Within days, their name should mean “looking at you pays.” Never use their name to scold — you want it to predict good things.

2. Sit

Hold a treat at your puppy’s nose and slowly move it up and back over their head. As their nose follows, their bottom will usually drop to the floor. The second it does: “Yes!”, treat. Once they are offering the sit reliably, add the word “sit” just before the hand movement, then gradually make the hand signal smaller.

3. Come

Crouch down, say their name and “come!” in your happiest voice, and reward them lavishly when they reach you — then let them go back to playing. Start indoors, with almost no distractions. Never call your puppy to you for anything unpleasant; recall has to be the best deal they are offered all day.

Common mistakes to skip

  • Repeating a cue five times. Say it once, then help your puppy succeed with your hand signal. “Sit-sit-sit” just teaches them to wait for the third one.
  • Training a wired or exhausted puppy. If they are mouthy, zooming, or glazed, they need a nap or a toilet trip, not a lesson.
  • Skipping management. Baby gates, a pen, and shoes put away prevent more bad habits than any amount of “no”.
  • Expecting a straight line. Skills appear, wobble, and reappear. That is normal learning, not defiance.

When to get help

See your vet promptly if your puppy stops eating, has vomiting or diarrhoea lasting more than a day, seems lethargic, coughs repeatedly, or you are worried about their health in any way — young puppies can go downhill quickly. Book a certified professional trainer (look for credentials such as CPDT-KA or IAABC) if you see fear that is not improving, guarding of food or toys, or biting that breaks skin. Getting help early is a sign of a good owner, not a failed one.

First-week toolkit checklist

  • Small, soft, pea-sized training treats (and part of their daily food ration for rewards)
  • A crate or exercise pen set up before arrival day
  • Enzymatic cleaner for inevitable accidents
  • Chew toys and a lick mat to redirect mouthing
  • A simple log of toilet trips, meals, naps and training wins — paper on the fridge works best
