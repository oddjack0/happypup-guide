Stop Leash Pulling: Loose-Leash Walking

Your dog is not pulling to spite you. They are pulling because, for their entire life, pulling has worked: the leash goes tight, and the interesting smell, tree, or other dog gets closer. Loose-leash walking means calmly teaching the opposite — a slack leash is what makes the walk move.

Why dogs pull

Three things keep pulling alive. First, dogs naturally walk faster than us, so a comfortable human pace already means restraint for them. Second, dogs have an opposition reflex: pressure against the chest or neck triggers an instinct to push into it, the same reflex sled dogs use. Third — the big one — pulling is self-rewarding. Every step taken on a tight leash teaches “this works”. That is good news, in a way: you do not need to overpower your dog, you just need to change what works.

Gear that helps

  • A well-fitted harness (ideally one that does not restrict the shoulders) spreads pressure safely. A front-clip attachment point can give you extra steering while you train — management, not a substitute for training.
  • A fixed-length leash, about 1.2–1.8 m (4–6 ft). Retractable leashes teach constant tension and are a poor fit for training.
  • A treat pouch. You will reward a lot at first; fumbling in pockets kills your timing.

We recommend against choke chains, prong collars and shock collars. They work by causing discomfort, veterinary and behaviour organisations such as the AVSAB advise against their use, and they risk creating fear or aggression around the very things your dog is straining towards. You do not need them for this method.

The stop-start method, step by step

  1. Start somewhere easy — indoors or in your garden. Walk a few steps; every time your dog is beside you on a loose leash, or glances up at you, mark it (“Yes!”) and treat. You are paying for position and attention.
  2. The leash goes tight: you stop. Plant your feet and wait, silently, like a tree. No jerking, no reeling them in. The walk simply stops being interesting.
  3. The leash slackens or your dog looks back: “Yes!”, treat, and walk on. That moment — your dog choosing to release the tension — is the exact behaviour you are buying. Reward it every time at first.
  4. Use direction changes. If your dog forges ahead repeatedly, cheerfully turn and walk the other way (“Let’s go!”). Turning makes you the interesting, unpredictable end of the leash.
  5. Keep sessions short and frequent. Five focused minutes on a quiet street teaches more than a forty-minute battle to the park. Give sniff breaks as rewards — sniffing is the whole point of the walk for most dogs.
The four-stage practice plan
StageWhereMove on when
1Indoors and gardenYour dog stays near your side and checks in often
2Driveway and the quietest stretch of your streetOnly occasional tight-leash moments, quickly recovered
3Your normal route, quieter times of dayMostly loose leash for a ten-minute walk
4Busier streets and park edgesLoose leash holds around mild distractions

Common mistakes

  • Jerking the leash back. It adds a tug-of-war your dog will happily play, and can hurt their neck.
  • Training only on the big daily walk, when your dog is bursting with energy. Tire the edge off with play first, and do training reps on separate short outings.
  • Everyone walking differently. If one person lets the dog tow them, the lesson dissolves. Agree the rules as a household.
  • Skipping stages. A dog who can do it in the garden has not failed when they cannot do it outside the dog park — the exam just got harder too fast.

When to hire a professional

Book a certified trainer (CPDT-KA, IAABC or similar) if your dog lunges, barks or fixates on other dogs or people — that is reactivity, a different problem from simple pulling; if your dog is strong enough to pull you over, which is a safety issue; or if several weeks of consistent practice bring no progress. Mention any sudden behaviour change to your vet as well, since pain can sit behind new pulling or reactivity.

Before-you-leave checklist

  • Harness fits snugly — two fingers under every strap
  • Fixed leash clipped on (retractable left at home)
  • Treat pouch stocked with high-value treats
  • Route chosen to match your current training stage
  • Five minutes of play first, if your dog is wound up
