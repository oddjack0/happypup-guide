Crate Training, Step by Step

Done gradually, a crate becomes the place your dog chooses for a nap: a safe den, a management tool while you can’t supervise, and a travel-ready skill for life. Done in a rush, it becomes a battleground. This plan takes the gradual route.

What a crate is — and is not

A crate is a bed with a door. It helps with potty training (dogs prefer not to soil where they sleep), keeps puppies out of mischief when you are in the shower, and gives nervous dogs a predictable retreat. It is not a punishment cell, not a substitute for exercise and company, and not somewhere a dog should spend most of their day. If you only remember one principle: everything good happens in or near the crate, and the door only closes when your dog is relaxed about it.

Pick the right size

Your dog should be able to stand up, turn around, and lie down stretched out — and not much more than that while potty training, or they may use one end as a toilet. For a growing puppy, buy for their expected adult size and use the divider panel most crates include to shrink the space as they grow. Add washable bedding, unless your puppy is a determined bedding-chewer, in which case a bare base is safer.

15–16 weeks3–4 hours
6 months and overUp to 4–6 hours for most adult dogs

A common rule of thumb for puppies is about one hour per month of age during the day. Night-time is different — sleeping puppies can usually go longer, especially with a last-thing toilet trip and one boring small-hours trip in the first weeks.

The step-by-step plan

  1. Days 1–2: the open-door café. Set the crate up where the family spends time, door propped open. Toss treats and favourite toys inside throughout the day. Feed every meal just inside the entrance, moving the bowl further back as your dog relaxes. No door closing yet.
  2. Days 3–4: the closing door. Start feeding meals fully inside and close the door while they eat, opening it as soon as they finish. Add a stuffed food toy or long-lasting chew and close the door for a few minutes while you stay in the room.
  3. Days 5–7: short stays. Build closed-door time in small jumps — 5, 10, then 20–30 minutes — while you move around the house normally. Give a chew every time. Let your dog out calmly, without a big fuss, before they get restless.
  4. Week 2 onwards: brief absences. Step out of the room, then out of the house for a minute or two, then ten. Keep departures and arrivals boring. Most dogs need days to weeks at each stage; the calendar is a guide, your dog’s body language is the truth.

Nights

For the first weeks, keep the crate in or near your bedroom — a puppy who can hear and smell you settles far faster, and you will hear the 2 a.m. toilet request. Night trips should be dull: on leash, out, toilet, praise quietly, back to bed. As your puppy starts sleeping through, you can move the crate to its permanent spot.

Common mistakes

  • Using the crate as punishment. Sending a dog to the crate in anger poisons the one association you are trying to build.
  • Letting them out mid-bark. If your dog is fussing, wait for even a few seconds of quiet before opening the door, so barking is not what “works”. That said, a puppy who is genuinely panicking should not be left to scream — go back a step in the plan instead (see below).
  • Skipping straight to long absences. The first time the door closes for two hours should never be the first time it closes at all.
  • Forgetting exercise first. A played-with, toileted, slightly tired dog crates happily; a wound-up one does not.

When to stop and get help

Normal protest — a few minutes of whining that fades — is different from panic. Stop and get professional help if your dog drools excessively, bites or claws at the bars, injures themselves, toilets in the crate despite being able to hold it, or cannot settle at all: these can be signs of separation anxiety or another underlying problem. Talk to your vet first to rule out pain or illness, and consider a certified behaviour professional (IAABC, or a veterinary behaviourist). Some dogs simply do better with a pen or a dog-proofed room, and that is a perfectly good outcome too.

Crate set-up checklist

  • Crate sized for standing, turning and stretching out (divider for puppies)
  • Placed where the family spends time, away from direct heat and draughts
  • Washable bedding (or none, for determined chewers)
  • Food-stuffing toys and safe chews reserved for crate time
  • Door propped open for the first two days — no exceptions
